No free lunch in ML
22
• No single algorithm is universally best for all problems.
• You cannot minimise both error types simultaneously—
reducing one usually increases the other.
• Due to threshold tuning (stringency)
• Lower threshold → catch more defaults (fewer false 
negatives) but more false alarms (more false positives).
• Higher threshold → fewer false alarms but more misses.
• The “best” trade-off depends on the economic cost function 
(e.g., in lending, missing a default is usually much costlier 
than wrongly rejecting a safe borrower).

Case Study: Funds Using ML Models
43
Background
In financial modelling, analysts often seek models that explain 
or predict returns, credit defaults, or frauds. With vast 
amounts of data—tick-by-tick prices, corporate financials, 
alternative datasets—it is tempting to fit highly complex 
models.
However, financial data is notoriously noisy, non-stationary, 
and subject to regime shifts. A model that fits historical data 
extremely well may fail in real markets. This is the essence of 
overfitting—capturing noise rather than signal.

Discussion: Using ML in Asset Management
44
Scenario
An asset management firm, AlphaQuant, designs a stock-selection model for its 
equity long/short fund.
Dataset: 15 years of daily data on S&P 500 constituents (prices, accounting 
ratios, analyst recommendations).
Target: Predict whether a stock will outperform the index over the next month.
Model chosen: A deep neural network with 12 hidden layers, trained on 
thousands of engineered features (momentum lags, valuation ratios, sentiment 
scores, etc.).
Performance in sample:
Training accuracy = 99%
Backtest Sharpe ratio = 3.5
Performance out of sample:
Test accuracy = 52% (close to random guessing)
Sharpe ratio collapses to 0.2

Discussion
45
What Happened?
Too many parameters vs. too little true signal: Finance rarely has strong 
stable patterns—models easily latch onto noise.
Data snooping bias: Repeated feature engineering and backtesting 
effectively mined spurious correlations (e.g., “stocks with tickers starting 
with M outperformed in 2014”).
Non-stationarity: Relationships that held in the past broke down as macro 
conditions and market structure evolved.
Lack of cross-validation: AlphaQuant optimised hyperparameters using 
the same period, inflating in-sample performance.
